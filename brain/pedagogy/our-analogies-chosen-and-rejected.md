**Rule.** Each core concept has one house analogy, chosen on purpose, and a short list of analogies we reject and why. An analogy is a teaching decision, not decoration — so it is recorded, not reinvented per lesson.

**Why our learner needs it.** A salesperson reasons from the analogy long after the lesson. If two lessons use different pictures for the same concept, the reader builds two mental models and trusts neither. Consistency across the course is worth more than a locally cuter comparison.

**How we choose.** An analogy earns its place only if it carries the *boundary* of the concept, not just its surface. It must also survive translation to Japanese without becoming a different image (see `every-analogy-must-survive-japanese`). If it implies something false when pushed one step, we reject it — beginners always push it one step.

**The house choices, and the rejected ones.**

- **Server = bank teller, not waiter.** The teller checks who you are and fetches from a vault you cannot enter. Rejected: *waiter/restaurant*, because it implies queueing and politeness, which are not the point, and learners over-extend it.
- **Frontend/backend = a trust line, not two job titles.** Rejected: *"frontend is looks, backend is logic"* — it is a description, not a boundary, and collapses the moment validation appears in both places.
- **Schema change = renovating a building, not editing a document.** Rejected: *"just add a column like adding a spreadsheet column"* — it hides that structure and data are different things.

**The misconception this pre-empts.** That any vivid comparison will do. A wrong analogy is worse than none: it teaches confidently and has to be un-taught later. When in doubt, name the concept plainly and add the analogy only once it clears the boundary test.
