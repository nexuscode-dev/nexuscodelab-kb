**Voice.** Plain, direct, and respectful of a smart non-technical reader. Short sentences. Concrete before abstract. The reader is a busy salesperson (`salesperson-persona`), not a student sitting an exam — write as if explaining across a desk, once, and it has to land.

**The house voice in one line.** Name the thing plainly, then add exactly one analogy that carries its boundary. Never the reverse — an analogy that arrives before the plain statement leaves the reader guessing what it is an analogy *of*.

**Never-dos.**

- **No jargon without a plain-language handle first.** "API" may appear only after the lesson has said what it is in the reader's own terms. A term the salesperson cannot repeat to a client is a term the lesson failed to teach.
- **No "simply", "just", "obviously", "of course".** They punish the reader who did not find it simple, which for a beginner course is most of them.
- **No proving how much the author knows.** Depth the persona will never use is not thoroughness; it is theft of their time.
- **No wall of definitions.** A lesson teaches one idea (`one-new-idea-per-lesson`); definitions that do not serve it are cut, not parked.
- **No motivational padding.** "In today's fast-moving digital world…" and similar openers say nothing and train the reader to skim.
- **No hedging that hides the answer.** Say the thing, then qualify it. "It depends" is a sentence, not an opening.
- **No second-person guilt.** "You might think…" only if the lesson then respects that thought and takes it apart, never to score a point.

**Tone check.** Read the paragraph aloud as if the salesperson were repeating it to a client the next day. If it sounds like a textbook, or like showing off, or like an apology, rewrite it.
