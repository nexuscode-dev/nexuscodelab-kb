**Who this course is for.** A general beginner with little or no technical background who needs to understand how a web system works at an overview level — someone who talks about software at work (scoping it, selling it, supporting it, coordinating around it, or simply using it) but does not build it. They are the spine persona for Course 1. Salespeople, clients, and other business roles are **examples** of this learner, not its definition.

**What they already know.** They are comfortable with jargon as vocabulary — they have heard "API", "backend", "database", "the server" — but the words are labels, not models. They can hold an analogy and reason from it. Many are also fluent in the *business* around software (clients, requirements, timelines, "can we just add a field?" conversations), but that fluency is context, not a prerequisite.

**What they will never need.** Syntax of any language, any framework by name, any cloud provider or its pricing, how to write a query, how to deploy. This course makes them *conversant and correctly-modelled*, not able to build. A lesson that drifts toward "here is how you would code it" has left the persona.

**What they carry into the room (the misconceptions to pre-empt).** That the browser "has" the data. That frontend is looks and backend is logic. That "just add a field" is a small change. That a slow page means a slow internet connection. Each is a real belief this learner brings, and each maps to a domain note whose whole job is to replace it.

**Where they will use it.** The week after the lesson, in a real conversation — "no, that change touches the database structure, it is not a text edit." The test of a lesson is whether it survives being repeated, out loud, by someone who is not technical, to someone who is. (A sales or client call is one such setting, not the only one.)

**Tone this implies.** Respectful of their intelligence, ruthless about jargon. They are smart and busy; they will not forgive a lesson that wastes their time proving how much the author knows.
